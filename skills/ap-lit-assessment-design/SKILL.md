# AP Literature assessment design

Check the current College Board CED and exam page before asserting exam specifications. Consult [the source and pattern register](references/patterns.md) for verified distinctions and editorial design decisions. This skill guides assessment design; it does not grant authority to publish work.

## Source and interpretation

Use a sustained literary stimulus before writing questions. Preserve verse lines, stanza boundaries, speakers, stage directions, meaningful punctuation, and the edition or translation. Separate textual observation, defensible inference, and outside context. Do not equate author, narrator, and speaker or diagnose a character as if the text were a clinical record. Explain how choices interact to shape an interpretation; device identification alone is not the objective.

Display enough context to distinguish competing readings without giving away the question’s answer. Number poem lines consistently and prose paragraphs editorially; never confuse responsive line wrapping with the source’s line numbers. Mark cuts and do not splice separated prose into an apparently continuous paragraph. Identify early editorial interventions in Dickinson and translators of drama. Keep public-domain source files and required license notices alongside readable excerpts.

## Multiple choice

Current public CED samples use four choices. Build one defensible inference and three plausible competing readings involving function, scope, perspective, sequence, or textual evidence. Explain each option from the actual passage. Anchor claims to stable line or paragraph references. Avoid trivia, generic device-definition drills, unsupported motive claims, and an obvious pattern of longer correct choices.

Match the requested unit of coverage. If every concept needs ten unique questions, give each concept ten distinct authored tasks and prohibit cross-concept reuse of canonical items. Sharing a source passage is compatible with unique questions. Renaming a repeated stem or changing an ID does not make a question unique. Preserve stable option ordering and version published changes to protect saved responses.

For writing-oriented concepts, use interpretation, evidence selection, and commentary evaluation as focused classroom practice. Do not suggest that AP Literature has AP Language’s separate revision-passage section. Short sets are practice, not calibrated exam simulations or score predictors.

## Free response

- Poetry analysis: provide the poem, author, source context, and an interpretive task about a complex response, relationship, or perspective. Preserve the whole relevant poem or explicitly mark a selection.
- Prose fiction analysis: provide an adequate excerpt and immediate situation; ask how literary elements and techniques develop a complex characterization, relationship, or experience. Drama may serve as a prose-fiction-analysis stimulus when appropriately presented.
- Literary argument: ask students to select a suitable work of fiction and explain how a specified feature contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole. This is not a personal-opinion essay and does not use a six-source synthesis packet. Suggested works are optional; students need accurate whole-work knowledge, not a supplied passage that pretends to replace reading.

Use the official thesis/evidence-and-commentary/sophistication dimensions (1/4/1) with linked full decision rules. Do not award complexity for ornamental concessions or equate a project percentage with an AP score. Scaffolds may offer a paraphrase check, an evidence-to-inference bridge, a possible alternative, and a revision checkpoint without dictating a single interpretation or paragraph formula.

## Creative-critical projects

Make the literary constraint consequential: voice, chronology, form, perspective, setting, a symbolic pattern, or a conflict must change what readers infer. Vary the artifact: poems, scenes, stories, epistolary sequences, dramatic scripts, illustrated editions, and narrative maps. Supply topic subjects rather than completed projects. Define audience, lengths, evidence, a visual relationship, self-analysis quoting the student’s choices, an interpretive argument, reader testing, substantive revisions, and reflection.

Use an analytic rubric with a distinct central literary criterion and a task-specific visual criterion. Assess reasoning and craft, not artistic expense. Provide text equivalents, low-material formats, and a documented alternate reading when peer testing is unavailable. Original compositions must be labeled; never attribute a student-created continuation to the historical author.

Review every key, quotation, anchor, skill link, prerequisite, and source attribution. Automated coverage tests cannot establish instructional or psychometric validity.
