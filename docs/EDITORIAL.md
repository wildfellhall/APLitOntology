# Editorial record

The source register is available in the site's Sources page and `skills/ap-lit-assessment-design/references/patterns.md`. College Board specifications were checked on November 1, 2026. The 90 nodes subdivide and extend the 31 official skills; they are editorial concepts, not 90 separately mandated College Board topics.

All practice questions, lessons, prompts, and project directions are original. Four-option questions interpret the displayed literary stimulus. The bank contains ten exclusive questions per concept; repeated passages support different reading tasks. Book practice gathers these same IDs, so saved responses remain shared across views. Every choice has an explanation. Questions are instructional practice and have not undergone psychometric validation.

The final editorial pass revised 92 keyed choices for clarity and length cues and 54 distractors with weak alternatives or insufficient explanations. Word-length patterns are a screening aid, not a validity measure. Further teacher review and student responses can reveal ambiguities that structural tests cannot detect.

Each project includes an original literary artifact, visual interpretation with accessible alternatives, source analysis and argument, self-analysis, testing and revision, reflection, attribution, and a task-specific 100-point analytic rubric. The four performance levels use common category descriptions and individualized accomplished criteria. Project scores do not map to AP scores.

The original Gutenberg downloads retain full licenses. Study excerpts identify source volume and location, distinguish supplied paragraph numbers from printed lines, preserve verse and speaker divisions, and identify omitted illustration captions. The source manifest records URLs, acquisition date, and hashes.

Edition cautions are visible beside the texts: Frankenstein uses the revised form; A Doll's House uses R. Farquharson Sharp's translation; Dickinson uses early edited posthumous collections; Blake's transcription prints “The Tiger”; collection publication dates are not assigned indiscriminately to individual poems or stories. Gray's 1751 Elegy is distinguished from its 1883 edited printing. Full reading copies include front matter and editorial material.

Historical context supplements close reading. A fictional household does not establish universal historical behavior, and a narrator's judgment does not become independent evidence merely because it appears in a primary text.
